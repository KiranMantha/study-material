Approach

What interviewers evaluate

Interviewers are rarely checking whether you know a specific product. They are looking for how you reason. Most rubrics cover these areas:

  • Requirements: do you ask clarifying questions and separate functional needs from non-functional ones such as latency, availability and durability?
  • Scale: do you estimate load and storage before designing, and use those numbers to justify choices?
  • Design: is the architecture clear, with sensible components and data flows?
  • Trade-offs: do you name what each choice costs, and when you would choose differently?
  • Depth: can you go deep on one or two parts, such as partitioning, caching or failure handling?
  • Communication: do you narrate your thinking, check in with the interviewer and adjust when they push back?

A good answer with clear reasoning and honest trade-offs often beats a fancy design with no justification.

Method

The answer framework

A repeatable order keeps you from jumping straight into boxes and arrows:

  1. Clarify requirements: who uses it, which features matter most, what scale, what latency and availability targets.
  2. Estimate: requests per second, storage and bandwidth, with peak factors.
  3. Define the interface: the main APIs, their inputs and outputs.
  4. Model the data: entities, access patterns, and whether the store should be relational or key-value.
  5. Draw the high-level design: the main components and the data flow between them.
  6. Deep dive: the hardest part of the problem, such as partitioning, hot keys, caching or consistency.
  7. Address bottlenecks and failures: what breaks first, what happens when a node or region fails, and how you recover.

Back-of-envelope estimation

Estimates do not need to be exact. They need to be the right order of magnitude, and they should drive a design choice. Useful constants:

Quantity Working value
Seconds in a day about 86,400 (round to 10^5 for quick math)
Average QPS daily requests ÷ 86,400
Peak QPS about 2 to 3 times the average, unless the prompt says otherwise
Text record about 1 KB for a typical row
Image or thumbnail tens to hundreds of KB; full-size photos are often several MB
Storage growth records per day × record size × retention days × replication factor

Worked example

Worked example: activity feed

Prompt: design a feed where users see posts from people they follow.

Estimates used (state your own assumptions in the interview): 100M daily users, 2 posts per user per day, average 200 followers per user. That gives about 2,300 post writes/s on average and roughly 7,000/s at peak.

Key decision: fan-out. There are two ways to build a feed:

  • Fan-out on write: when a user posts, copy the post into each follower's feed. Reads are fast, but writes are expensive, and a celebrity with millions of followers makes a single post extremely costly.
  • Fan-out on read: assemble the feed when it is requested, by reading recent posts from everyone the user follows. Writes are cheap, but reads are slower.

A common answer is a hybrid: fan out on write for ordinary accounts, and merge posts from high-follower accounts at read time. Kafka fits as the event backbone: posts are published to a topic, and fan-out workers consume it.

Talking points interviewers often probe:

  • Partition key: partitioning posts by author keeps each author's posts in order. Ask yourself whether ordering across authors matters for the feed.
  • Hot partitions: a celebrity account concentrates traffic on one partition. Mitigate with the hybrid model, or with extra sub-keys for the hottest accounts.
  • Duplicates: consumers use at-least-once delivery, so feed inserts must be idempotent. Key each feed entry by post ID so a retry does not add it twice.
  • Consistency: a post can appear in feeds a few seconds late. Say that is acceptable, which means choosing availability over strict consistency for feeds, as in the CAP section of the system design guide.
  • Failures: if fan-out workers fall behind, consumer lag grows. Scale workers up to the partition count, and alert on lag.
  • Storage: estimate the feed store by feeds × entries per feed × entry size, and cap each feed at a fixed number of recent posts.

Kafka and trade-offs

Kafka questions to expect

These come up often. Use the Kafka guide for the underlying details. Strong answers state the trade-off in one line and then explain it.

  • Why Kafka instead of a traditional queue? Kafka keeps events in a replayable log, so many independent consumers can read the same data, and a new consumer can replay from the beginning. Traditional queues usually delete a message once it is consumed.
  • How do you choose a partition key? Pick a key that you need ordering for and that spreads traffic evenly. A key with very few values, or a very busy value, creates hot partitions.
  • How do you get ordering? Ordering holds only within a partition. Send related events to the same partition with the same key. Cross-key ordering needs a different design, or it may not be needed.
  • How do you avoid losing data? Use acks=all, a replication factor of 3, a suitable min.insync.replicas, and idempotent producers. Describe what happens if a broker fails during a write.
  • What are the delivery guarantees? At-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once. Explain which you need and what it costs. Be clear that exactly-once applies within Kafka, and that external side effects still need idempotent handling.
  • How do you scale consumers? Add consumers up to the partition count. More partitions add parallelism but cost more broker resources, and changing the count can move keys to new partitions.
  • What does consumer lag tell you? How far a group is behind the end of the log. Rising lag means the consumers cannot keep up. Investigate processing speed, hot keys and rebalances.
  • What happens during a rebalance? Partitions move between consumers, so processing pauses for those partitions. Cooperative rebalancing reduces the pause. Keep processing fast so you stay inside the poll interval.

Trade-off talking points

Interviewers want to hear the cost of each choice. Use this as a starting point:

Decision Option A Option B When to pick which
Consistency Strong: writes wait for agreement Eventual: replicas catch up later Strong for payments and inventory; eventual for feeds and counters
Communication Synchronous request-response Asynchronous via a queue or log Sync when the caller needs the answer now; async to absorb spikes or decouple
Data store Relational: joins, transactions Key-value or wide-column: scale, simple access Relational when the access pattern is varied; key-value when it is known and high volume
Scaling Vertical: bigger machine Horizontal: more machines, sharding Vertical first for simplicity; horizontal once the ceiling is near or availability matters
Caching Cache-aside: read through the app Write-through: write to cache and store Cache-aside for read-heavy data; write-through when reads must see recent writes
Feed building Fan-out on write Fan-out on read Write for normal accounts; read for very large follower counts (hybrid)

Common mistakes

  • Drawing boxes first. Without requirements and numbers, the design has no basis, and the interviewer cannot judge it.
  • Skipping estimates. Scale changes the answer. A system for 1,000 users and one for 1 billion are very different.
  • Naming technologies without reasons. "We use Kafka" says little. "We use Kafka because we need a replayable log that several consumers read" shows understanding.
  • Ignoring failure. Explain what happens when a node, a broker, a region or a dependency fails.
  • Hiding trade-offs. Every choice has a cost. Naming it is a strength.
  • Over-engineering. Start simple, then add complexity where the numbers demand it.
  • Going silent. Think out loud, ask questions, and accept hints without getting defensive.

Practice

Practice questions

These are representative prompts for practice, not a list from any company. For each one, work through the framework above and time yourself.

  • Design a URL shortener. (Key generation, read-heavy caching, storage estimate.)
  • Design a rate limiter for an API. (Token bucket, distributed counters, sharding.)
  • Design a chat messaging system. (Connection handling, ordering, delivery receipts, storage.)
  • Design a video streaming service. (CDN, transcoding pipeline, upload flow.)
  • Design a distributed job scheduler. (Queues, retries, exactly-once concerns, leader election.)
  • Design an analytics pipeline for click events. (Kafka ingestion, partitioning, stream processing, batch storage.)
  • Design a search autocomplete system. (Trie or prefix index, caching, freshness vs cost.)
  • Design a ride-matching service. (Geospatial indexing, sharding by region, real-time updates.)

Four-week study plan

  • Week 1: framework and estimation. Practice the constants until they feel automatic. Do two short designs.
  • Week 2: core building blocks. Caching, load balancing, replication, sharding, queues. Use the system design guide as a reference.
  • Week 3: Kafka and streaming. Use the Kafka guide and the questions above. Practice explaining partitioning and delivery guarantees out loud.
  • Week 4: full mock interviews. Time each one to 45 minutes, then review the recording or your notes for weak areas.

Practise aloud. Interviews test how you explain your reasoning, and silent study does not build that skill.
